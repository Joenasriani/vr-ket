## Research question

What similarities and differences are defensible when comparing **VR immersion/presence** with **ketamine-associated dissociative experiences**, and how much direct evidence exists for combining VR with ketamine-related treatment contexts?

## Evidence boundary

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower than the original page implied:

- VR can alter presence, embodiment, attention and perception through mediated sensory input and interaction.
- Ketamine is an NMDA-receptor antagonist with dose- and context-dependent dissociative/psychedelic effects and established clinical research in anesthesia and depression.
- These phenomena can share high-level concepts such as altered self/world perception, but they are produced by very different mechanisms and should **not** be presented as equivalent states.
- Direct clinical evidence specifically testing **VR + ketamine as one combined therapeutic intervention** is limited. A PubMed search for `"virtual reality" ketamine` returns mostly studies where the two appear as separate treatment modalities or in different contexts, not a mature evidence base demonstrating synergy.
- This repository therefore treats the comparison as a **conceptual literature question**, not as evidence that VR is a substitute for ketamine, that one reliably reproduces the other, or that combining them improves clinical outcomes.

- Petrucci ABC et al. *Ketamine versus electroconvulsive therapy for major depressive episode: An updated systematic review and non-inferiority meta-analysis.* Psychiatry Research (2024). PMID 38865906: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38865906/
- Man DWK. *Virtual reality-based cognitive training for drug abusers: A randomised controlled trial.* Neuropsychological Rehabilitation (2020). This involved young ketamine users, but it evaluated VR cognitive training rather than VR administered together with ketamine. PMID 29734923: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29734923/
- *Virtual Reality as a Moderator of Psychedelic-Assisted Psychotherapy.* PMID 35310225: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35310225/ — relevant to the broader question of immersive environments around altered-state therapy, but not by itself evidence for a VR-ketamine treatment effect.
